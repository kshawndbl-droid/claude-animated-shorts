# The Man Who Sold the Eiffel Tower (tall tale style)

Target: about 65 s, about 170 words. The narrator is a folksy storyteller who tells a true story like a tall tale: deadpan, a wink in his voice, short punchy lines and quick cuts. Every fact is checked below. Nothing is exaggerated beyond the sources; the tall-tale flavour comes only from the way it is told.

## Script

1. In 1925, a man sold the Eiffel Tower. Small problem... it wasn't his.
2. His name was Victor Lustig. A con man with dozens of fake names, and a smile that could sell sand in the desert.
3. He read that the tower cost Paris a fortune in repairs. So he printed some fake government letterhead,
4. called himself a deputy director, and invited the city's biggest scrap-metal dealers to a fancy hotel.
5. The pitch? Top secret. Paris is tearing down the tower, and thousands of tons of iron go to one lucky bidder.
6. One dealer, André Poisson, bit. Hook, line and tower. He paid up, cash in hand.
7. And Lustig? He skipped town with a suitcase full of money.
8. Poisson was so embarrassed that he never told the police he'd been tricked into buying the Eiffel Tower.
9. So Lustig came back... and tried to sell it AGAIN. This time a dealer smelled a rat, and Lustig fled to America.
10. There he charmed five thousand dollars out of Al Capone and sold fake "money-printing" boxes. Then he started printing real fake money.
11. That last one got him caught. He was sent to Alcatraz. The man who talked his way into selling a tower couldn't talk his way off a rock.
12. And the tower? Still standing. Still not for sale.

## Fact check

- 1925, posing as a government official, letters to scrap-metal dealers, the story that Paris would scrap the tower because of costly maintenance: Eiffel Tower official site ("mid-1920s") and Smithsonian ("by 1925").
- Meeting at the Hôtel de Crillon, "Deputy Director" title, about 7,000 tons of metal: Smithsonian. The official site says 7,300 tons, so the script says "thousands of tons".
- Buyer André Poisson; he paid, then was too embarrassed to press charges: both sources. The amount differs between sources, so the script gives no sum.
- Second attempt failed, he left France for the US: both sources (Smithsonian says he suspected a dealer had told the police).
- Capone gave him $5,000 for "honesty", the "money box" scam, counterfeiting, caught in 1935, sentenced to Alcatraz: Smithsonian. Imprisonment in 1935: official site.
- "Dozens of aliases": Smithsonian.
- He died in prison in 1947. The script does not joke about his death and does not mention it.

Sources: [Eiffel Tower official site](https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/history-and-culture/who-sold-eiffel-tower), [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-smoothest-con-man-that-ever-lived-29861908/)
